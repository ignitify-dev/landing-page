Plans

The isolation is the same on every plan.

Most of this ladder is quantity — more VMs, more tasks, more throughput. A few rungs go further and unlock something your code cannot do on the plan below; those are the ones worth moving for. Every limit comes from the tier you are on, granted whole and enforced by the platform rather than configured per tenant, so what you can run is a property of your plan and not of who asked. What happens when you hit a limit

You get a 429 with the reason, the limit, and what you actually used — and upgrading fixes it.

What you won’t get is a 429 because the host is busy. That case returns 503, or the run is simply queued and dispatched when a slot frees. A transient full host is never reported as a plan problem, so you’re never told to upgrade when all you needed was to wait.

Changing plans

Your plan is pinned at the version you were granted. Plans are never edited in place — a price or limit change publishes a new version, and existing customers stay on theirs until they choose to move.

Grandfathering isn’t a policy anyone has to remember here; it’s how the tier system is built.
